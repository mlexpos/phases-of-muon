Proof strategies

This section gives an informal overview of the proof strategies and how they fit together. The arguments are grouped by the methods they share: the resolvent calculation leads to the drift and volatility kernels, which enter the comparison with deterministic dynamics; asymptotic analysis of these kernels and a Volterra equation then give the predictions for learning rates and times to accuracy. The following sections are organized by result. They state the precise hypotheses and conclusions, identify the corresponding Lean formalizations and their status, and give a proof sketch for each result. The strategies described here explain the connections between those proofs; the supplement contains the complete proofs.

SJ.5 Deterministic equivalent for the EMA Gram resolvent

Every kernel of the spectral family is a spectral integral against the resolvent of the Gram matrix of the momentum buffer, and this engine computes that resolvent (Section SB). It works in the frozen-state model of Definition SA.3, in which the state is held fixed and every gradient in the buffer is recomputed at it with its own independent batch. The normalized buffer is then
\[ \Mt=\sum_{j\ge0}\emaw j\,G_{t+1-j},\qquad \emaw j=(1-\bmom)\bmom^j,\qquad G_{t+1-j}=\frac1B\sum_{a=1}^Bd_{ja}\,\Sigmaout^{1/2}(z_{ja}\tensor w_{ja})\Sigmain^{1/2}, \]
with independent standard Gaussian vectors \(z_{ja},w_{ja}\) and residuals \(d_{ja}=z_{ja}^\T\Ahat w_{ja}\), where \(\Ahat=\Sigmaout^{1/2}\Dt\Sigmain^{1/2}/\sqrt{2\cR(t)}\) is the normalized error, of Frobenius norm one. The engine assumes the proportional regime and that the error is diffuse, \(\norm{\Ahat}\to0\): no singular direction of the error carries a fixed fraction of the risk. Its output is that, uniformly for \(s\) in compact subsets of \((0,\infty)\) and in operator norm (Theorem SB.2),
\[ \EE\big(\Mt\Mt^\T+s\Id\big)^{-1}\longrightarrow\Rbar(-s)=\big(s\Id+\tilde s_1(-s)\,\Sigmaout\big)^{-1}, \]
with the same statement for \(\Mt^\T\Mt\) and the limit \(\Rtbar(-s)=\big(s\Id+s_1(-s)\Sigmain\big)^{-1}\), where \(\tilde s_1,s_1\) solve the weighted fixed-point system of Definition SB.1; normalized traces against bounded test matrices converge in mean square (Corollary SB.1).
The resolvent equation is first written exactly through one deleted sample. The buffer is a sum of rank-one terms, one per sample, \(\Mt=\sum_{j,a}\mathsf h_{ja}\,\mathsf u_{ja}\tensor\mathsf v_{ja}\) with \(\mathsf h_{ja}=\emaw jd_{ja}\), \(\mathsf u_{ja}=\Sigmaout^{1/2}z_{ja}/\sqrt B\) and \(\mathsf v_{ja}=\Sigmain^{1/2}w_{ja}/\sqrt B\), so the identity \(\Id-sR=R\Mt\Mt^\T\) for \(R=R(-s)\) is a sum over samples of \((\mathsf hR\Mt\mathsf v)\tensor\mathsf u\). Removing one sample changes \(\Mt\) by a rank-one matrix, and the Woodbury formula for a \(2\times2\) block computes the change exactly (Lemma SB.4). With \(M_0\) the buffer without the sample, \(R_0\) its resolvent and \(\mathsf X_0=R_0M_0\),
\[ \mathsf h\,R\Mt\mathsf v=-\alpha_3\,R_0\mathsf u+\alpha_1\,\mathsf X_0\mathsf v,\qquad \alpha_1=\frac{\mathsf h(1+\mathsf h\mathsf C)}{\Lambda},\quad \alpha_3=-\frac{s\mathsf h^2\mathsf V}{\Lambda},\quad \Lambda=(1+\mathsf h\mathsf C)^2+s\mathsf h^2\mathsf U\mathsf V, \]
where \(\mathsf U=\mathsf u^\T R_0\mathsf u\), \(\mathsf V=\mathsf v^\T\widetilde R_0\mathsf v\) and \(\mathsf C=\mathsf u^\T\mathsf X_0\mathsf v\). The deleted resolvents do not depend on the deleted sample, so after taking expectations each term is a Gaussian computation in one sample.
The coefficients then become functions of the residual of the deleted sample alone. The scalars \(\mathsf U\) and \(\mathsf V\) are quadratic forms of a Gaussian vector independent of \(R_0\), so they concentrate, with errors \(O(B^{-1/2})\), at the expected normalized traces \(\sigma_B=\frac1B\Tr(\Sigmaout\EE R)\) and \(\tilde\sigma_B\), and \(\mathsf C\) concentrates at \(0\) (Lemma SB.11); the traces themselves fluctuate by \(O(B^{-1/2})\), by the Efron–Stein inequality (Lemma SB.10). For a sample of age \(j\) this gives, in mean square (Lemma SB.12),
\[ \alpha_1\approx\frac{\emaw j\,d}{1+\emaw j^2q_B\,d^2},\qquad \alpha_3\approx-\frac{s\,\emaw j^2\,\tilde\sigma_B\,d^2}{1+\emaw j^2q_B\,d^2},\qquad q_B=s\,\sigma_B\tilde\sigma_B , \]
and the age of a sample enters only through its squared weight in the denominator.
Gaussian integration produces the weight function, and diffuseness removes the cross term. In the term carrying \(\alpha_3\) one needs \(\EE[\psi(d)\,z\tensor z]\) with \(\psi(d)=d^2/(1+xd^2)\) and \(x=\emaw j^2q_B\). Stein’s identity gives the exact formula
\[ \EE\big[(z\tensor z-\Id)\psi(d)\big]=\Ahat\,\EE\big[(w\tensor w)\psi''(d)\big]\,\Ahat^\T, \]
whose Frobenius norm is at most \(6\norm{\Ahat}\) (Lemma SB.8). The residual \(d\) is asymptotically standard Gaussian because \(\norm{\Ahat}\to0\) (Lemma SA.7), so \(\EE\psi(d)\) tends to \(\Jfun(x)\). In the term carrying \(\alpha_1\) the response is \(\EE[f(d)\,w\tensor z]=\Ahat^\T\EE f'(d)+O(\norm{\Ahat}^2)\), of Frobenius norm of order one but of operator norm at most \(\norm{\Ahat}\), which vanishes. Summing over ages gives, in operator norm ((SB.44)),
\[ \Id-s\,\EE R-a_B\,\EE R\,\Sigmaout\longrightarrow0,\qquad a_B=s\,\tilde\sigma_B\,\Fw(q_B),\qquad \Fw(q)=\sum_{j\ge0}\emaw j^2\,\Jfun(\emaw j^2q), \]
that is, \(\EE R\approx(s\Id+a_B\Sigmaout)^{-1}\). Taking the trace against \(\Sigmaout\) closes the loop: \((\sigma_B,\tilde\sigma_B,a_B,c_B)\), with \(c_B\) the input-side analogue of \(a_B\), solves the weighted system up to vanishing residuals.
An approximate root is close to the root. The weighted system has exactly one nonnegative solution, and it is stable under approximate roots uniformly over spectral measures of bounded mass and support (Theorem SB.1(iii)). Hence \(a_B\to\tilde s_1\), and the resolvent identity gives \(\EE R\to\Rbar\). The Gaussian limit of \(d\) and the replacement of the coefficients are qualitative, no rate is assumed for \(\norm{\Ahat}\to0\), and the stability is proved by compactness, so the engine gives no rate. The system depends on the whole weight sequence: with \(\sw p=\sum_j\emaw j^p\), the EMA weights with \(\bmom>0\) have \(\sw4/\sw2^{\,3}=(1+\bmom)^2/(1+\bmom^2)>1\), so no single effective batch size \(B/\sw2\) reproduces \(\Fw\) beyond its value at \(q=0\) (Remark SB.2).

SJ.6 Drift: the expected regularized update

This engine computes the mean of the update in the frozen-state model (Section SC), which is the input of every drift kernel. It works in the setting of Section G.1 and uses its output. It produces a deterministic matrix \(\Gtildebar_\ridge\), whose entries in the eigenbases of the covariances are multiples of the corresponding entries of \(\Dt\), with, for normalized ridges in a fixed interval \([\ridge_-,\ridge_+]\subset(0,\infty)\) (Theorem SC.2),
\[ \sup_{\ridge\in[\ridge_-,\ridge_+]}\norm{\EE\,\Qridge\ridge(\Mt)-\Gtildebar_\ridge}_\F\longrightarrow0 . \]
The norm is the unnormalized Frobenius norm, the norm in which (SC.13) controls the drift errors of all modes together.
The update is an integral of the mixed resolvent along the negative axis. For a singular value \(\sigma_k\) of \(\Mt\), the scalar integral \(\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty\sigma_k(\sigma_k^2+\ridge+u^2)^{-1}du\) equals \(\sigma_k(\sigma_k^2+\ridge)^{-1/2}\), the filter of the regularized polar map, so (Lemma SA.3)
\[ \EE\,\Qridge\ridge(\Mt)=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty\mathcal H(\ridge+u^2)\,du,\qquad \mathcal H(s)\defeq\EE\big[(\Mt\Mt^\T+s\Id)^{-1}\Mt\big]. \]
It suffices to find a deterministic equivalent of \(\mathcal H(s)\) in Frobenius norm, uniformly for \(s\) in compact sets, and a bound that controls the integral at large \(s\).
The mixed resolvent solves a linear response equation. Deleting one sample as in Section G.1, now through the identity \(\mathsf hR\mathsf u=\alpha_1R_0\mathsf u-\alpha_2\mathsf X_0\mathsf v\) with \(\alpha_2=\mathsf h^2\mathsf U/\Lambda\), writes \(\mathcal H\) exactly as a sum over ages of a source term carrying \(\alpha_1\) and a feedback term carrying \(\alpha_2\) (Lemma SC.6). In the source term the Gaussian response of a sample with residual function \(f\) is
\[ \EE\big[f(d)\,z\tensor w\big]=\Ahat\,\EE f'(d)+O\big(\norm{\Ahat}^2\big), \]
and multiplying by \(\Sigmaout^{1/2}\) and \(\Sigmain^{1/2}\) turns \(\Ahat\) into the mean gradient
\[ \EE\Gt=\Sigmaout\Dt\Sigmain/\sqrt{2\cR(t)}. \]
By Stein’s identity \(\EE f'(d)\to\Jfun(\emaw j^2q)\), so the weights now enter to the first power, through \(\Lw\). In the feedback term \(\alpha_2\) is replaced by its conditional mean, whose expected sum over ages, \(b_B(s)\), tends to \(\sigma\Fw(q)\). The result is the response equation (Proposition SC.3)
\[ \mathcal H(s)\big(\Id+b_B(s)\,\Sigmain\big)=\Lw(q)\,\Rbar(-s)\,\EE\Gt+o(1)\quad\text{in Frobenius norm.} \]
Since \(s(\Id+\sigma\Fw(q)\Sigmain)=s\Id+s_1\Sigmain=\Rtbar(-s)^{-1}\) by the fixed-point system, the candidate below solves the same equation exactly with \(b_B\) replaced by its limit, and comparing the two equations shows that \(\mathcal H(s)\) is close to it (Theorem SC.1(i)):
\[ T(s)=s\,\Lw\big(q(-s)\big)\,\Rbar(-s)\,\EE\Gt\,\Rtbar(-s). \]
Because the Frobenius norm is not normalized by the dimension, every replacement must cost \(o(1)\) there, which is more than the trace statements of Section G.1 give. The delicate replacement is the covariance between the conditional mean of \(\alpha_2\) and the deleted mixed resolvent. It is expanded along a martingale over the other samples; replacing one of them changes the buffer by rank two, which moves the conditional mean by \(O(\emaw j^2/B)\) (Lemma SC.4), and the covariance is \(O(\emaw j^2B^{-1/2})\) in Frobenius norm (Lemma SC.5).
The integral is split at a cutoff. The exact identity, without any asymptotic replacement, bounds \(\norm{\mathcal H(s)}_\F\le C/s\) for \(s\ge1\) at every dimension and for every state, and \(\norm{T(s)}_\F\le C_\Sigma/s\) (Theorem SC.1(ii)). With \(\Gtildebar_\ridge=\frac2\pi\int_0^\infty T(\ridge+u^2)\,du\) this gives, for every \(\mathsf R\ge1\),
\[ \norm{\EE\,\Qridge\ridge(\Mt)-\Gtildebar_\ridge}_\F\le\frac2\pi\Big[\mathsf R\sup_{\ridge_-\le s\le\ridge_++\mathsf R^2}\norm{\mathcal H(s)-T(s)}_\F+\frac{C}{\mathsf R}\Big], \]
and the dimension limit followed by \(\mathsf R\to\infty\) gives the output.
The candidate acts entrywise in the eigenbases. Since \(\Rbar(-s)\) and \(\Rtbar(-s)\) are functions of \(\Sigmaout\) and \(\Sigmain\),
\[ u_i^\T T(s)v_j=\frac{s\,\Lw(q(-s))\,\mu_i\lambda_j}{(s+\tilde s_1(-s)\mu_i)(s+s_1(-s)\lambda_j)}\cdot\frac{u_i^\T\Dt v_j}{\sqrt{2\cR(t)}}, \]
and integrating at \(s=\ridge+u^2\) gives \(u_i^\T\Gtildebar_\ridge v_j=\mathfrak d(\mu_i,\lambda_j)\,u_i^\T\Dt v_j/\sqrt{\cR(t)}\) with the kernel (SC.4). The same argument applied to the buffer truncated to its first \(J\) ages shows that, after the dimension limit, ages beyond \(J\) contribute a vanishing amount as \(J\to\infty\) (Proposition SC.1); the dynamics uses this.

SJ.7 Volatility and the lag kernel

The volatility is computed in the frozen-state model of Definition SA.3: the error is held at a state whose normalized error \(\Ahat\) has vanishing operator norm, and the momentum buffer \(M_{t'}=\sum_{j\ge0}\emaw jG_{t'+1-j}\) is a stationary average of independent batches at that state. The updates \({\mathsf S}_{t'}=\Qridge\ridge(M_{t'})\) are then stationary, and \({\mathsf S}^{\mathrm c}_{t'}\) is the update minus its mean. For matrices \(F,G\) diagonal in the eigenbases, \(C_\ell[F,G]=B^{-1}\EE\Tr[F{\mathsf S}^{\mathrm c}_tG\,{\mathsf S}^{\mathrm c\,\T}_{t+\ell}]\) is the covariance of two updates \(\ell\) steps apart; with \(F=u_i\tensor u_i\) and \(G=v_j\tensor v_j\) it is that of the entries of mode \((i,j)\). The engine produces the forcing measure \(\zeta_\ridge\). Its atom at a point \((\mu,\lambda)\) is the deterministic long-run covariance of the update tested on the eigenspaces of \(\mu\) and \(\lambda\), and each mode \((i,j)\) contributes the mass \(\mathfrak v_{ij}/B\) at \((\mu_i,\lambda_j)\). The engine shows that the covariance measure of \(T\) consecutive updates, divided by \(BT\), is within an explicit error of its long-run value and within \(o(1)\) of \(\zeta_\ridge\), and that \(\mathfrak v_{ij}\) is a series of nonnegative double integrals of a closed-form kernel.
Proof sketch.
Over a block of \(T\) steps the updates accumulate their lag covariances, which decay geometrically in the lag. Expanding the square of \(S_T=\sum_{t'\le T}{\mathsf S}^{\mathrm c}_{t'}\) over pairs of times, stationarity makes all pairs at lag \(\ell\) equal. This gives an exact identity, and the geometric decay bounds its distance to the long-run variance \(\Gamma[F,G]=C_0[F,G]+2\sum_{\ell\ge1}C_\ell[F,G]\) (Theorem SD.2(i),(iii)):
\[ \begin{aligned} \frac1{BT}\,\EE\Tr\big[F\,S_T\,G\,S_T^\T\big]&=C_0[F,G]+2\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\Big(1-\frac\ell T\Big)C_\ell[F,G]\\ &=\Gamma[F,G]+O\Big(\frac{\bmom^T}{1-\bmom}+\frac{\bmom}{T(1-\bmom)^2}\Big). \end{aligned} \]
The decay comes from the recursion \(M_{t+\ell}=\bmom^\ell\Mt+\sum_{r<\ell}\emaw rG_{t+\ell+1-r}\). Replacing \(\Mt\) in it by a copy built from independent batches gives a buffer with the law of \(M_{t+\ell}\), independent of \(\Mt\), at distance \(\bmom^\ell\) times the difference of two independent buffers. The regularized map is \(\ridge^{-1/2}\)-Lipschitz in Frobenius norm, so \(\abs{C_\ell}\le\mathsf K_\star\bmom^\ell\). The error term of the display holds also in total variation for the covariance measure of \(T\) consecutive updates (Theorem SD.8(iv)), the form the dynamics use.
At each fixed lag, the covariance is unchanged in the limit when every residual \(d=z^\T\Ahat w\), which depends on its own sample, is replaced by an independent Gaussian weight. Call a filter an odd function applied to the singular values of a buffer: \(\Qridge\ridge\) is the filter \(x/\sqrt{x^2+\ridge}\), and it is an average of the resolvent filters \(x/(x^2+s)\), which give \((KK^\T+s\Id)^{-1}K\) (Lemma SA.3). Adding one sample is a rank-one change of each buffer. By the Woodbury formula, with a \(2\times2\) matrix to invert, the change of a tested product of two resolvents is a function of \(d\) and of quadratic forms that concentrate at normalized traces, so it depends on the sample only through the law of \(d\), which is asymptotically Gaussian because \(\norm{\Ahat}\to0\). Summed over the samples, one at a time, the errors vanish (Lemma SD.6).
In the resulting model, which has no state, the lag covariances have deterministic limits in which only tree-shaped Gaussian pairings survive. For polynomial filters the tested product of the two buffers is a polynomial in Gaussian variables, and its expectation is a sum over pairings, each gluing the cyclic product into a connected graph. Pairings whose graph is a tree contribute order one and the others \(O(1/B)\) (Lemma SD.8). The tree values are products of normalized traces \(B^{-1}\sum_i\mu_i^p\), \(B^{-1}\sum_j\lambda_j^p\) and of moments of the weights, hence deterministic, and polynomial approximation extends the limit to all odd bounded continuous filters (Lemma SD.10). The resolvent filters give the lag kernel \(\mathfrak k_\ell[F,G](s,u)\), and the filter of \(\Qridge\ridge\) gives the deterministic covariance \(\overline C_\ell[F,G](\ridge,\ridge)\), the double integral of \(\frac4{\pi^2}\mathfrak k_\ell[F,G](\ridge+x^2,\ridge+y^2)\) over \(x,y>0\) (Theorem SD.3 and Theorem SD.6). With the geometric decay, \(\Gamma\) is within \(o(1)\) of \(\overline\Gamma=\overline C_0+2\sum_{\ell\ge1}\overline C_\ell\), without a rate (Theorem SD.4(iv)).
The trees can be summed, and the lag kernel is an explicit expression in the solution of the fixed-point system of Figure 2 at two spectral parameters and in two scalar functions of the momentum. In every tree, the subtrees hanging off the path from the vertex carrying the test \(F\) to the vertex carrying the test \(G\) involve one buffer only, and they sum to its one-time deterministic equivalent (Section G.1): the branch \((\sigma,\tilde\sigma,\tilde s_1,s_1,q)\) at \(-s\) for the earlier buffer and, primed, at \(-u\) for the later one (Lemma SD.18). Only batches on the path are shared; a batch of age \(j\) enters the two buffers with weights \(\emaw j\) and \(\bmom^\ell\emaw j\). The sum over paths is a geometric series of \(2\times2\) matrices, and, first for large \(s,u\) and then by analyticity for all \(s,u>0\) (Theorem SD.5),
\[ \begin{gathered} \mathfrak k_\ell[F,G](s,u)=\frac{su\,b_\ell\,\Tr\big(F\Sigmaout\Rbar(-s)\Rbar(-u)\big)\Tr\big(G\Sigmain\Rtbar(-s)\Rtbar(-u)\big)}{B^2\,\Delta_\ell(s,u)},\\ b_\ell=\sum_{j\ge0}\EE\frac{\bmom^\ell\emaw j^2g^2}{(1+q\emaw j^2g^2)(1+\bmom^{2\ell}q'\emaw j^2g^2)}, \end{gathered} \]
where \(\Rbar(-s)=(s\Id+\tilde s_1\Sigmaout)^{-1}\), \(\Rtbar(-s)=(s\Id+s_1\Sigmain)^{-1}\), and \(\Delta_\ell>0\) is the determinant of the \(2\times2\) series, built from the branch, the traces of the same form with \(F=\Sigmaout\) and \(G=\Sigmain\), \(b_\ell\) and a companion scalar \(e_\ell\). The lag enters only through \(b_\ell\) and \(e_\ell\), and \(b_\ell\) carries the factor \(\bmom^\ell\). With spectral projections as tests the kernel factors into multiplicities and a nonnegative mode kernel, \(\mathfrak k_\ell[P_\mu,P'_\lambda](s,u)=r_\mu t_\lambda B^{-2}\,\mathfrak h_\ell(\mu,\lambda;s,u)\) for the spectral projections \(P_\mu,P'_\lambda\) of \(\Sigmaout,\Sigmain\) with ranks \(r_\mu,t_\lambda\), and the atom of \(\zeta_\ridge\) at \((\mu_i,\lambda_j)\) splits into equal mode coefficients (Corollary SD.3, Proposition SD.3)
\[ \mathfrak v_{ij}(\ridge)=\frac1B\Big(\mathfrak c_0(\mu_i,\lambda_j;\ridge)+2\sum_{\ell\ge1}\mathfrak c_\ell(\mu_i,\lambda_j;\ridge)\Big), \]
where \(\mathfrak c_\ell(\mu,\lambda;\ridge)\ge0\) is the double integral of \(\frac4{\pi^2}\mathfrak h_\ell(\mu,\lambda;\ridge+x^2,\ridge+y^2)\) over \(x,y>0\).

SJ.8 From the algorithm to the deterministic dynamics

This is the argument of Theorem SE.1, sketched in full after that theorem. It takes in the drift kernel \(\mathfrak d\) (Section G.2), the forcing measure \(\zeta\) with the covariance of consecutive updates (Section G.3), and an initial error that is diffuse: the normalized error \(\widehat A_0\), of Frobenius norm one, has vanishing operator norm. It produces the comparison, up to a time \(T_\star\), of the spectral energy measure \(\chi(\Delta_t)\), which places \(\CMscr Q_{ij}\) at \((\mu_i,\lambda_j)\), with the solution of the deterministic measure equation ((SE.9)).
The argument has four ideas. The algorithm is compared, over blocks of \(L=\lfloor B^{1/8}\rfloor\) steps, with the model in which the state is held at its value at the start of the block and the momentum buffer is a stationary average of independent batches; because a test of the energy measure is a quadratic form in the error, its expected change over a block has an exact expansion ((SE.28)). The two leading terms of that expansion are the drift and the volatility, and because the block is long the momentum correlations of consecutive updates are summed in full. The motion of the state inside a block changes the expected update only through a causal response, which Stein’s identity reduces to projections that are small because the error is diffuse (Proposition SE.4). Finally, diffuseness persists, by an operator-norm bound on the mean update that holds at every state and a matrix martingale inequality (Lemma SE.24). Gronwall’s inequality and routine estimates close the comparison.

SJ.9 Power-law and large-batch asymptotics

This engine computes the half-anisotropic coefficients \(\mathfrak d_i\) and \(\mathfrak v_i\) when \(\Sigmain=\Id_N\) and \(\Nout=\Nin=N\), in two regimes: the thin batch \(N/B\to\infty\) with a power-law spectrum, and the oversampled batch \(B_{\rm eff}>3(1+\bmom)^2N\) with an arbitrary spectrum, where only the drift is computed. It takes in the scalar fixed-point system of the deterministic equivalent (Section G.1) and the integral formulas for the drift and the volatility (Section G.2 and Section G.3), and it produces explicit profiles. At each finite dimension the coefficients are deterministic functions of the spectrum, of \(B\) and of \(\bmom\); the zero-ridge coefficient is their limit as \(\ridge\downarrow0\), and nothing is claimed about the algorithm at zero ridge.
With an isotropic input side, the fixed-point system reduces to a single scalar, the participation \(m\). Put
\[ m_N(\Lambda)=\frac1B\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{\Lambda\mu_i}{1+\Lambda\mu_i},\qquad \Lambda_N\defeq m_N^{-1},\qquad f_i(m)=\frac{\Lambda_N(m)\mu_i}{1+\Lambda_N(m)\mu_i}. \]
Each eigenvalue enters with a soft indicator of \(\mu_i\ge1/\Lambda\), so \(Bm_N(\Lambda)\) is a soft count of the output directions above the level \(1/\Lambda\), and \(\sum_if_i(m)=Bm\). On the branch at the spectral parameter \(-s\) the participation equals \(r_\bmom(q)\defeq q\Fw(q)\), which counts, up to a bounded error, the past batches that the normalized update whitens (Lemma SG.1(iii)), and \(s\) fixes \(m\) through the exact relation \(s=(\gamma-m)m/(Q_m\Lambda_N(m))\), with \(Q_m=r_\bmom^{-1}(m)\); this is a decreasing bijection of \((0,\gamma)\) onto \((0,\infty)\) (Proposition SG.1). In this coordinate the drift is a single integral and the zero-lag volatility is \(f_i\) itself ((SG.7)):
\[ \mathfrak d_i(\ridge)=\frac{\sqrt2}{\pi\gamma}\int_0^\infty \frac{\Lw(Q_{m(\ridge+u^2)})}{\Fw(Q_{m(\ridge+u^2)})}\,f_i\big(m(\ridge+u^2)\big)\,\dif u, \qquad \volraw_i(\ridge)=f_i\big(m(\ridge)\big). \]
The volatility is a double integral over two participations with an explicit lag kernel ((SG.22)). With momentum \(r_\bmom\) is unbounded, so \(m\to\gamma\) as \(s\downarrow0\), and at zero ridge the zero-lag coefficient of every row is one, \(\volraw_i(0)=1\) (Proposition SG.2 and Proposition SG.4).
The drift integral splits at participation of order one into a head, where few past batches are whitened, and a tail, where many are. In the head \(q\) is small, the weight functions take their small-argument values \(\Fw\approx\sw2\) and \(\Lw\approx1\), and the integrand is a Lorentzian in \(u\) of width \(\sqrt{\gamma\sw2\mu_i}\). When the row is resolved, that is \(m_N(1/\mu_i)\to0\), the head part of the drift is (Lemma SG.11)
\[ \sqrt2\,\mathfrak d_i^{\le M}\sim\sqrt{\frac{\mu_i}{\gamma\sw2}}=\sqrt{\frac{\mu_iB_{\rm eff}}N}. \]
In the tail the weight function \(\sqrt q\,\Lw(q)\) oscillates with the geometric spacing of the weights and has no limit, but its average in the participation is exact. With \(\mathcal A(m)\defeq\sqrt{Q_m}\Lw(Q_m)/E(m)\) and \(E(m)\defeq Q_mr_\bmom'(Q_m)\) (Lemma SG.2),
\[ \sup_{t\ge m_0}\Big|\int_{m_0}^t\big(\mathcal A(m)-\sqrt{2\pi}\big)\,\dif m\Big|\le C_{\bmom,m_0}. \]
In the variable \(q\) each past batch contributes \(\int_0^\infty x^{-1/2}\Jfun(x)\,\dif x=\pi\,\EE\abs g=\sqrt{2\pi}\) once it is whitened, and \(m\) counts the whitened batches up to a bounded error. An integration by parts then replaces \(\mathcal A\) by \(\sqrt{2\pi}\) against any slowly varying weight ((SG.9)). This is where the constant \(c_0=\sqrt{2\pi}/\pi=\EE\abs g\) of the profiles comes from.
For a power law the participation is a Riemann sum of a continuum function, and the small-argument law of that function separates the cases \(\alpha<1\), \(\alpha=1\) and \(\alpha>1\). With \(V=c_N\Lambda N^{-\alpha}\) one has \(m_N(\Lambda)=\gamma S_N(V)\), where \(S_N(V)=N^{-1}\sum_jV/(V+(j/N)^\alpha)\) is within \(1/N\) of \(S_\alpha(V)=\int_0^1V(V+t^\alpha)^{-1}\dif t\), so that \(\Lambda_N(m)=(N^\alpha/c_N)V_N(m/\gamma)\) with \(V_N=S_N^{-1}\) ((SG.42), Lemma SG.6). As \(V\downarrow0\) (Lemma SG.5),
\[ S_\alpha(V)\sim\frac V{1-\alpha}\ (\alpha<1),\qquad S_1(V)\sim V\log\frac1V,\qquad S_\alpha(V)\sim K_\alpha V^{1/\alpha}\ (\alpha>1), \]
with \(K_\alpha=\pi/(\alpha\sin(\pi/\alpha))\). For \(\alpha>1\) this says that a participation \(m\) resolves about \(Bm/K_\alpha\) rows: \(c_N\Lambda_N(m)\sim(Bm/K_\alpha)^\alpha\) when \(Bm\to\infty\) and \(m/\gamma\to0\) (Lemma SG.6(iv)). The amplitude \(c_N\) multiplies the drift by \(\sqrt{c_N}\) and leaves the volatility unchanged (Lemma SG.4), so the proofs take \(c_N=1\). A uniform statement over the rows reduces, by passing to subsequences, to row sequences along which \(i/N\) and the relevant ratios converge.
In the oversampled regime the branch is the momentum-free profile equation up to a factor that stays in \([1-\epsilon_{\rm os},1]\), with \(\epsilon_{\rm os}=3(1+\bmom)^2N/B_{\rm eff}\). In the rescaled variables \(\hat s=s/(\sw2\gin)\) and \(b=\tilde s_1(-s)/(\sw2\gin)\), where \(\gin=N/B\), the branch satisfies exactly (Lemma SH.4)
\[ \frac b{\widehat F}+m_{\hat\nu}(\hat s/b)=1,\qquad \widehat F=\frac{\Fw(q)}{\sw2}\in(0,1],\qquad m_\nu(c)=\int\frac{x\,\nu(\dif x)}{c+x}, \]
where \(\hat\nu\) is the empirical spectral distribution of \(\Sigmaout\); this is the same soft count as above, since \(m_N(\Lambda)=\gamma\,m_{\hat\nu}(1/\Lambda)\). The coupling gives \(q\Fw(q)<\gin\), which bounds \(q\), and the Gaussian inequality \(\Jfun(x)\ge1/(1+3x)\) (Cauchy–Schwarz with \(\EE g^4=3\)) then keeps \(\widehat F\) and \(\Lw\) above \(1-\epsilon_{\rm os}\) for every \(s\) (Lemma SH.3). A monotone comparison places \(b\) between \(\widehat Fb_0\) and \(b_0\), where \(b_0\) is the root at \(\widehat F=1\), and hence the drift integrand within the factors \((1-\epsilon_{\rm os})^{\pm1}\) of the integrand of the profile \(\mathfrak d^{\rm os}\) (Lemma SH.5, Theorem SH.1). The substitution \(c=\hat s/b_0\) turns the profile equation into \(\Upsilon_\nu(c)\defeq c(1-m_\nu(c))=\hat s\), and one integration by parts gives the exact representation (Lemma SH.8)
\[ \mathfrak d^{\rm os}_\nu(\mu)=\frac{\sqrt2}\pi\,\mu\int_0^\infty\frac{\sqrt{\Upsilon_\nu(c)}}{(c+\mu)^2}\,\dif c =\sqrt{\frac\mu2}\;\chi_\nu(\mu),\qquad \chi_\nu(\mu)=\int_0^\infty\varpi(y)\sqrt{1-m_\nu(\mu y)}\,\dif y, \]
with \(\varpi(y)=\frac2\pi\sqrt y\,(1+y)^{-2}\). Here \(\varpi\) is a probability density and \(1-m_\nu(c)\) is a soft fraction of the spectrum below \(c\), so the efficiency \(\chi_\nu(\mu)\in(0,1]\) averages the square root of the fraction of the spectrum below the level of the mode, on a logarithmic scale around it (Remark SH.2). The results on head, packed and tail modes are bounds on this average.

SJ.10 Volterra analysis and the phase map

This is the argument of Theorem SI.7, and the common part of every result of Section M. It takes in the deterministic row model of that chapter: the row recursion (14) in continuous time, with weights \(\mu_i\), drifts \(\mathfrak d_i\), volatilities \(\mathfrak v_i\) and initial row energies that are fixed numbers (Definition SI.1), run at the matched step \(\lr=2\sqrt\epsilon/\mathcal S\). It produces an exact formula for the time \(t_{T\epsilon}\) as a prefactor times a dimensionless integral, lower and upper bounds for that integral, and, for the power-law arrays, its order in each range of exponents.
Proof sketch.
The risk of the model solves a scalar equation once time is measured in units of the effective step. In the row equations the drift of every row is divided by \(\sqrt{\fRisk}\), so the natural time is \(s=\clock(t)=\int_0^t\lr\,\fRisk(t')^{-1/2}\,\dif t'\). In this time each row solves a linear equation driven by \(\sqrt Q\), where \(Q(s)\) is the risk at time \(s\), and summing the rows with weights \(\mu_i/2\) gives exactly (Theorem SI.1 and Theorem SI.2)
\[ \begin{gathered} Q=F+k*\sqrt Q,\qquad t(s)=\frac1\lr\int_0^s\sqrt{Q(v)}\,\dif v,\\ F(s)=\tfrac12\sum_i\mu_i\fQ_i(0)e^{-2\mathfrak d_is},\qquad k(s)=\tfrac\lr2\sum_i\mu_i\mathfrak v_ie^{-2\mathfrak d_is}. \end{gathered} \]
The forcing \(F\) is the risk the model would have without noise, and the kernel \(k\), of total mass \(\lr\mathcal S/2\), feeds the volatility back in. Monotone iteration gives a solution, unique by the comparison principle below, and its limit is \(\lr^2\mathcal S^2/4\), which is \(\epsilon\) at the matched step; there the kernel has mass \(\sqrt\epsilon\).
The time to a level is the integral of \(\sqrt Q\) up to the first passage, and it is squeezed between the forcing and any function that dominates the equation. Since \(k*\sqrt Q\ge0\), the risk is at least the forcing, so it cannot reach a level before the forcing does, and \(t_a\ge\lr^{-1}\int_0^{s_F}\sqrt F\), with \(s_F\) the first time \(F\le a\) (Theorem SI.3). Conversely, a positive function \(U\) with \(U\ge F+k*\sqrt U\) lies above \(Q\): where \(Q>U\) the difference is bounded by a fixed multiple of its own convolution with \(k\); since \(k\) has small mass on short intervals, this forces it to vanish step by step (Lemma SI.3).
For the power-law arrays a change of time scale separates the dimension from the accuracy. With the time scale \(T_0^j\) the decay rates \(2\mathfrak d_iT_0^j\) become \(2i^{-q_j}\), and \(Y_j(u)=Q_j(T_0^ju)\) solves \(Y_j=f_{N,q_j}+\sqrt\epsilon\,g_j*\sqrt{Y_j}\), where \(f_{N,q}(u)=\frac12\sum_{i\le N}i^{-p}e^{-2ui^{-q}}\) and \(g_j\) is a decreasing kernel of mass one; neither depends on \(B\) (Proposition SI.4). If \(\hat u_j\) is the first time \(Y_j\le T\epsilon\), then exactly ((SI.30) and (SI.26))
\[ t^j_{T\epsilon}=\frac{\mathcal S_jT_0^j}{2\sqrt\epsilon}\,\mathsf I_j,\qquad \mathsf I_j\defeq\int_0^{\hat u_j}\sqrt{Y_j(u)}\,\dif u,\qquad \frac{\mathcal S_{\text{s-SGD}}T_0^{\text{s-SGD}}}{\mathcal S_{\text{s-SVD}}T_0^{\text{s-SVD}}}=D_N . \]
The order of the clock integral \(\mathsf I_j\) is decided by whether \(\sqrt{f_{N,q_j}}\) is integrable uniformly in \(N\). The forcing lies between two power laws (Lemma SI.7), the lower one for \(N\ge4\) and \(u\le(N/4)^q\):
\[ A_p(1+u)^{-2r_q}\le f_{N,q}(u)\le C_{p,q}^2(1+u)^{-2r_q},\qquad r_q=\frac{p-1}{2q} . \]
Write \(\mathsf I_j=\int_0^{\hat u_j}\sqrt{f_{N,q_j}}+\mathcal E_j\), where \(\mathcal E_j\ge0\) is the noise part. When \(r_j\le1\), suppose the first passage occurs before a horizon \(H\le(N/4)^{q_j}\). There the lower envelope holds, and \(f_{N,q_j}(\hat u_j)\le T\epsilon\) gives \(1+\hat u_j\ge(A_p/T\epsilon)^{1/(2r_j)}\). Integrating the envelopes then gives (Lemma SI.10)
\[ \int_0^{\hat u_j}\!\!\sqrt{f_{N,q_j}}\ \begin{cases} \text{lies between }c\,\epsilon^{-(1-r_j)/(2r_j)}\text{ and }C_{p,q_j}I_{r_j}(H),&r_j<1,\\[2pt] =\dfrac{\log(1/\epsilon)}{2\sqrt{8q_j}}+O\big(1+\log_+((1+H)\sqrt\epsilon)+HN^{-q_j}\big),&r_j=1,\\[4pt] \longrightarrow J(p,q_j)\defeq\int_0^\infty\sqrt{f_{\infty,q_j}(u)}\,\dif u\quad(N\to\infty,\ \epsilon\downarrow0),&r_j>1, \end{cases} \]
with \(I_r(x)=\int_0^x(1+w)^{-r}\dif w\), so that \(C_{p,q}I_r(H)\) is of order \(\epsilon^{-(1-r)/(2r)}\) when \(H\) is of order \(\epsilon^{-1/(2r)}\). Since \(r_{\text{s-SVD}}=(p-1)/\alpha\) and \(r_{\text{s-SGD}}=(p-1)/(2\alpha)\), the boundary \(r_j=1\) is \(\texp=1\) for SignSVD and \(\texp=\alpha+1\) for SignSGD.
The noise part is controlled by comparison with \((h+\sqrt\epsilon)^2\), and checking the kernel condition is what produces the windows. Take for \(h\) a power-law envelope of \(\sqrt{f_{N,q_j}}\) that decreases to \(\sqrt\epsilon\) at a horizon \(H\). If \(g_j*h\le2h\) or \(g_j*h\le\sqrt\epsilon\) on \([0,H]\), then \((h+\sqrt\epsilon)^2\) dominates the equation, the first passage occurs before \(H\), and \(\mathcal E_j\) is at most a fixed multiple of \(\epsilon^{(r_0-1)/(2r_0)}\), where \(r_0\le r_j\) is the exponent of the envelope and \(r_0>1\) exactly when \(r_j>1\) (Lemma SI.9). For \(r_j<1\) this is of the order of the main term, so these models get orders; for \(r_j>1\) it tends to zero, and the time has an equivalent. (For SignSVD at \(\alpha=2\), \(\texp>1\), Lemma SI.18 adds a correction term to the square and bounds the noise part by a constant that tends to zero.) The kernel condition holds by small local mass of a decreasing kernel, by an integrable power tail, or by a small kernel height, each on a range of times that the window of the theorem keeps above the horizon (see the note to Theorem SI.7).
